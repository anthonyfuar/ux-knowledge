# Law of Prägnanz

## In one line
Least mental effort wins: complicated image / one open to several readings → people settle on plainest version they can. Use when simplifying icons, logos, shapes or busy composition, explaining why complex figure reads as one simple form, or citing umbrella principle behind Gestalt grouping laws.
- Aliases: Prägnanz; umbrella principle behind Gestalt grouping laws. Class: effect / principle.
- Word: German for pithiness; connotes salience, conciseness + order.
- Umbrella role: spontaneous assembly of scattered parts into complete things + regular arrangements = what Prägnanz names + what grouping principles set out to explain.
- Theme: Perception (perception and grouping). Most affects: icon + logo simplicity, shape clarity, less visual noise in complex figures.

## Applies when / does not apply when
|Applies when|Watch out when|
|---|---|
|image ambiguous / complicated|measuring "simplicity": hard to operationalize, while closure + continuity easy to point to|
|several intricate shapes could fuse into one whole|wanting model of how perception processes input: cite grouping laws as reliable descriptive tendencies only|
|icon, logo / key object needing fast recognition|cited evidence = demonstration figure, not controlled UX experiment|

## Decision rules
- Simple shapes + compositions; reduce shapes + layouts to simple unified forms.
- Expect viewers to make one form from complex visuals.
- If you can draw figure plain or intricate → draw plain: better remembered + less visual effort.
- Plan merged outline, not only parts: viewers take in cluster of intricate shapes as one whole (eye's shortcut).
- Icons + key objects: clean, distinctive silhouettes; clear outline recognized faster than detailed shape w/ ambiguous contour.
- If several grouping cues in play → make them agree + test; do not rely on fixed cue-strength ranking.
- If screen reads as undifferentiated clutter → evident hierarchy, dividers, modules w/ backgrounds / borders.

### Citing
- Cite grouping laws as reliable descriptive tendencies, not as model of how perception processes input.
- Most grouping evidence = demonstration figures, not controlled UX experiments → say so when citing, e.g. 72-circle example.
- Proximity + Similarity: no originating person / study beyond 1923 grouping formalization → attribute to Gestalt psychologists as group.
- 1910 observation marks start of Gestalt principles overall; not test of simplicity itself.
- Gestalt therapy = separate field; argued to have no substantive relation to Gestalt perception research → do not cite as perceptual evidence.

## Why it works

### Mechanism
- Overload defence: to keep information overload away, vision searches any intricate shape for regularity + plainness.
- Simplest reading costs least cognition → default; one ordered form aids memory + prevents overload.
- Global regularity can override local cues: "simplest" sometimes = splitting outline into circle, square + triangle, sometimes = merging fragments into one whole.
- Perception assembles simpler whole than one physically present; any motion read into static-flash sequence = illusory.
- Apparent motion = mind builds one moving object from static flashes.
- Gestaltists treated Prägnanz as fundamental law; other grouping laws try to specify it.
- Grouping origin contested: inborn (Gestalt school, whose principles rest on this: people arrive w/ rulebook for sorting what they sense) vs learned from real-object statistics (1975 view).
- Customary grouping-cue taxonomy counts five families; alphabetically: Closure, Connectedness, Continuity, Proximity, Similarity.

### Origin
- Traces to 1910 observation by psychologist: watching railroad-crossing lights blink in turn, perceived one light in motion.
- Published as phi phenomenon in 1912, treated as founding of Gestalt psychology.
- Almost all graphic design leans on principles describing how vision works; they trace back to single 1910 moment.
- Perception + grouping laws from Gestalt psychologists. Proximity + Similarity formalized 1923; Common Region introduced 1992; Uniform Connectedness introduced 1994.

## Misreadings and limits
- Wrong: ranking grouping cues by fixed strength (e.g. connectedness always wins) → Right: such rankings come from demonstrations + winner depends on cue magnitude; make cues agree + test.
- Wrong: treating all grouping principles as special cases of good form → Right: this gives economy but less clarity.
- Wrong: citing 1910 observation as proof simpler = better → Right: marks start of Gestalt principles overall, not test of simplicity itself.
- Wrong: citing Gestalt therapy as perceptual evidence → Right: separate field, argued to have no substantive relation to Gestalt perception research.
- Wrong: grouping laws = model of how perception processes input → Right: reliable descriptive tendencies; innate vs learned contested.
- Limit: simplicity hard to operationalize, while closure + continuity easy to point to.
- Limit: perception assembles simpler whole than what physically exists; motion perceived in static flashes = illusion.

## Check questions
[A] = answerable from artifact itself (screen, prototype, spec).
- [A] Can screen read as a few simple shapes? Look for: busy, irregular compositions.
- Icons + key objects: clean, distinctive silhouettes, not detailed shapes w/ ambiguous contours?
- All grouping cues agree on one reading, + tested?

## Symptoms → fixes
|Symptom|Likely cause (laws)|Fix|
|---|---|---|
|Layout reads as undifferentiated clutter|Chunking, Common Region, Prägnanz|evident hierarchy, dividers, modules w/ backgrounds or borders|
|Icon misread or recognized slowly|detailed shape w/ ambiguous contour|clean, distinctive silhouette|
|Complex figure remembered poorly|intricate, not plain form|reduce to simple unified form|
