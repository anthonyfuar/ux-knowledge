# Peak-End Rule

## In one line
Experience judged not by sum or average of its moments; two feelings mostly decide: one at most intense moment (peak) + one at end. Use when designing confirmation, success, wait, error, 404 / outage moments, deciding how flow ends, or reading retrospective satisfaction scores.
- Alias: peak-end theory. Class: effect / principle; often described as tightly linked to Cognitive Bias + as belonging to psychology.
- Tendency, not law.
- Theme: Expectations (Expectations and emotion). Most affects: confirmation + success screens, wait-state design, error + 404 fallbacks.
- When citing it → name both halves (peak + end); never cite ending alone.

## Applies when / does not apply when
|Applies when|Does not apply / weak when|
|---|---|
|episode w/ clear start + finish|no clear episode; Skip tap / dismissed tour (neither peak nor end: experience continues into product)|
|question = remembered judgment + decision to return|question = abandonment mid-experience (in-the-moment frustration still causes it)|
|peak = most intense moment of either valence, positive or negative, not merely "worst" one|—|
|end = real close of episode|mid-journey moments mislabelled as "end"|
|episodes studied so far: lab pain, medical procedures, waits, material goods, media|day-long episodes (one research line found they do not follow it → take care w/ day-long services); long services + immersive media (verify first)|
|most people|some groups, e.g. restrained eaters in one study ignored peak + end, used prior beliefs|

Weighting shifts:
- Peak effects fade faster over time than end effects.
- Goals + expectations shift weighting: end marking goal completion weighs more.
- Price expectations change dominant moment: premium offers must impress early; cheap ones gain most from good ending.
- Argued: mood at evaluation time also colors recall; retrospective ratings track peak, end + person's current mood.
- In extended experiences recency beats primacy.

## Decision rules

### Find + design the peaks
- Design peaks + ending deliberately; they deserve most careful design.
- Locate emotional peaks w/ journey map (three zones):
  1. Lens: whose view: research-grounded persona, scenario, persona's expectations.
  2. Experience: timeline of phases + actions, w/ mindset layer (motivations, questions, pain points, thoughts) + emotion line where peaks show.
  3. Insights: internal owners, metrics, opportunities.
- Delight target = moments product already does best (fun, worthwhile, most useful) → map where they fall, then shape.
- High-stakes commit (e.g. send w/ no undo) = emotional peak → let brand personality calm it: reassuring tone, light joke, drawing, restrained motion; matters most for first-timers.
- Soften high-stakes peaks + celebrate ending.
- In onboarding → lever = person's first real success.
- Broaden-and-build theory: positive emotions broaden thought + action + build lasting resources → positive peaks may widen exploration, not only please.

### Plan the negative peaks
- Plan negative peaks: waits, errors, 404 pages, payment declines.
- Bad experiences leave sharper memories than good ones → plan for negative peaks (stated principle, no study cited).
- One bad peak (e.g. wait / outage) can dominate memory of whole experience.
- Prepare failure fallbacks in advance (security flaws, outages, design choices excluding some customers).
- Design against negative peaks + discount rosy retrospective surveys.
- Avoid consecutive failures or hard steps + never end on one.
- Session stopping at worst moment (crash, failed step) locks that moment into memory.
- Error + outage states: explain cause, give realistic recovery time, carry no blame, avoid alarm styling when no action possible.

### Waits
- Treat waits as potential negative peaks.
- Unavoidable wait → answer three things on screen: how long + how figure derived (operational transparency); step under way, each shown (goal-gradient); what to watch meanwhile, something informing + diverting (idleness aversion).
- Make waits finish faster than expected; do not let them stall at end.
- Make progress indicators accelerate toward end.

### Endings
- End flows positively: confirm success, show relevant details, acknowledge effort.
- Soften last step of painful flows even if it adds a little time; end flows calm + positive.
- Better ending can outweigh added duration: longer experience can win if it ends better; duration + averages matter less than peak + end, + less than expected.
- Duration neglect → shortening painful flow does little if its worst moment + ending stay bad.
- Extra mildly good content at end can dilute memory of experience.
- Close mixed feedback on its most acceptable part.
- Recency dominates memory of extended pleasure; reminders of earlier good moments can rebalance it.
- Present bad state (e.g. open, unresolved support frustration) recolors earlier good product memories.
- Never pad or lengthen bad flow to game recall → fix worst moment + ending instead.

### Measuring
- Collect in-the-moment ratings (experience sampling) alongside retrospective surveys.
- Never read end-of-journey score as record of each step.
- Time surveys away from unresolved problems apt to color recall.
- Retrospective surveys come out rosier than reality (fading affect bias) → discount them. Related recall bias: people overrate own past choices in recall (choice-supportive bias).

### Citing
- Find mechanism first, then law; if no law fits → cite heuristic.
- Cold-water study: say most participants picked longer trial; do not quote percentage w/o checking 1993 paper.
- Peak-end = tendency: verify it for long services + immersive media.
- Research line spans many years w/ different claims → match each year to its claim: cognitive bias work 1972 + 1974; attention capacity 1973; prospect theory 1979; framing 1981; peak-end 1993, 1996 + 2003; experiencing vs remembering self 2005; 2011 popular book on System 1 / System 2 (labels borrowed from earlier researchers).

## Why it works

### Mechanism
- Memory bias (type of cognitive bias), related to recency effect: recall favors emotion-charged events over neutral ones.
- Would person repeat it, recommend it? → both answers follow approximate mean, held in memory, of two feelings: at peak + at close.
- Memory never stores episode's full chronology, only stills standing for it.
- Intense moments remembered better than mild ones; people fail to correct for atypicality of extremes.
- In extended experiences, recency beats primacy: each new moment interferes w/ memory of earlier ones.
- Ending counts partly because it fixes episode boundary; boundary defines what counted as peak (2000 argument).
- Memory rebuilt from current state (see widowed-participants row in Evidence).

### Terms
|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Snapshot model of remembered value (1993)|rooted in representativeness heuristic: a few prototypical moments stand for whole episode; stored value ~ mean of most intense + last moment. Total pleasantness + length not forgotten, only not used. Snapshots chosen by intensity, not valence → sharp low qualifies as readily as high|
|Duration neglect (1993)|experience length barely affects remembered value: summing moment quality takes effort memory avoids. About memory, not experience: no license for long processes|
|Temporal monotonicity|choosing longer cold-water trial violates it|
|Experiencing self vs remembering self (2005)|experiencing self lives moment by moment; remembering self holds stored story driving future choices|
|Fading affect bias|unpleasant feelings fade faster than pleasant ones → retrospective surveys come out rosier than reality. Negative moments still stick: sharp negative peak stored more vividly + weighs more, while episode's overall tone softens over time|
|Choice-supportive bias (2000 research)|people overrate own past choices in recall|
|James Dean effect (2001 vignette study)|wonderful life cut short judged happier than same life plus five more mildly pleasant years; abrupt excellent endings beat longer good ones|
|Streak-end rule (2022, field data on volunteer crisis counselors)|runs of hard tasks + hard most-recent task predicted quitting out of proportion; runs of bad events act like peaks|
|Broaden-and-build theory|positive emotions broaden thought + action + build lasting resources|

- From 1993 paper reporting lab experiment (cold water, see Evidence).

## Product cases
|Case|What happened|Strength|Lesson|
|---|---|---|---|
|Uber Express POOL (launched early 2018)|adds wait between request + match plus short walk to pickup point → straighter routes, cheaper trips; riders used to fast matching + door-to-door pickup cancelled during wait at high rate vs other ride products|—|treat waits as potential negative peaks|
|Uber Express POOL wait-screen redesign|in A/B test, rate of cancellations after ride request fell 11 percent (11%) once wait screen gained arrival estimate w/ info icon on its derivation, every hidden stage (locating car, matching co-riders heading same way) + motion = operational transparency, goal-gradient, idleness aversion|company-reported, 2019, written up 28 January 2019 in company engineering blog post on applied behavioral science; single in-house experiment, not general effect size|ease unavoidable waits w/ these three levers|

## Misreadings and limits
- Wrong: only peak + end matter (as popular summaries overstate) → Right: rough tendency; day-long episodes, some VR content + some groups do not follow it, + most memorable moment can predict better.
- Wrong: citing ending alone → Right: name both halves, peak + end.
- Wrong: averaging satisfaction across journey → Right: peaks + endings dominate memory, negative ones most.
- Wrong: attaching Peak-End to behavior it does not explain → Right: Peak-End = emotional peak + real episode end, not mid-journey Skip tap.
- Wrong: Skip tap / dismissed tour = "end" of experience → Right: episode continues into product; onboarding lever = designed first success + clean ending of real task.
- Wrong: shortening mediocre flow + expecting better memory (duration neglect) → Right: duration barely affects remembered value; improve worst moment + ending.
- Wrong: duration never matters → Right: retrospective ratings track peak + end more than total; duration neglect = about memory, not experience, + no license for long processes (in-the-moment frustration still causes abandonment).
- Wrong: citing Doherty Threshold for 0.1 s pressed state or for double-charge prevention → Right: Doherty Threshold = system response time; double-charge prevention = error prevention + idempotency.
- Wrong: padding / prolonging bad experience for better memory → Right: fix worst moment + ending; nicer memory does not justify extra real cost.
- Limit: evidence from lab pain + medical procedures; transfer to UX = inference.
- Limit: peak effects fade faster than end effects; mood at evaluation (argued) + current state also color recall.

## Check questions
[A] = answerable from artifact itself (screen, prototype, spec).
- [A] Peak + final moment deliberately designed? Look for: bare confirmation modals at high-stakes steps; flows ending on flat / negative screen.
- [A] Flows end w/o exit pop-up, upsell or hook? Look for: exit-intent offers; "please don't go" dialogs; upsells on confirmation screens.
- [A] Negative peaks (waits, errors, 404s, outages) planned? Look for: default error pages; unexplained waits.
- [A] Error + outage states explain cause, give realistic recovery time, carry no blame + avoid alarm styling when no action possible? Look for: red generic "try later" errors w/o estimate.
- [A] (Peak-End, streak-end) Consecutive failures / hard steps avoided, especially at end? Look for: repeated validation errors; final rejection.
- Claimed "end" = real close of episode, not Skip tap / dismissed tour?
- In-the-moment ratings collected alongside retrospective surveys?

## Symptoms → fixes
|Symptom|Likely cause (laws)|Fix|
|---|---|---|
|Experience remembered badly despite good average|worst moment + ending dominate (Peak-End)|improve worst moment + ending; add positive confirmation screen|
|Good session soured at last step|exit hooks, harsh errors, stalled end (Peak-End)|no exit pop-ups or upsells; errors + outages calm, blame-free + time-estimated; progress bars accelerating at end|
|Card declined / payment times out|Working Memory; Peak-End negative peak; error-recovery heuristic|keep all fields + clear only security code; give plain-word reason + offer another payment method; on timeout, check order status before allowing retry|
|People cancel during wait|wait = negative peak|show every step, estimate + its method, something informative to watch; finish faster than expected|
|Retrospective scores rosier than in-the-moment signals|fading affect bias|add in-the-moment ratings; discount rosy surveys; time surveys away from unresolved problems|
|People quit after run of hard steps|streak-end|break up consecutive failures or hard steps; never end on one|

## Humane bound
- Bounded by Finite: experience has edges + clean ending.
- Bounded by Empowering: leaving as easy as joining.
- Bounded by Transparent.
- Ethical limit: nicer memory does not justify extra real cost; prolonging painful procedure improves recall but adds pain. Even within research line behind clinical studies, doubt raised whether patients would accept added pain purely for better memory.
- Turned against people: endings manufactured to cover bad experience.
- Turned against people: bad flow padded or lengthened to game recall.
- Turned against people: hooks at exit, e.g. exit-intent "please don't go" pop-ups, discounts + upsells at cancellation, logout or leaving.
- Turned against people: artificial delight added to moments not already valuable.
- Further reading for this bound: memorable moments + endings patterns (entry "End well"); defaults + choice architecture patterns (entry "Symmetric exit"); influence vs manipulation (does designed peak / ending serve person or exploit them?).
