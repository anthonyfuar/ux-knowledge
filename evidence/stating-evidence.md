# Stating evidence

## Use when
- Before stating study, figure, case or law's origin in design rationale, review comment / stakeholder argument.
- Rules for quoting studies, numbers + origins accurately.
- Two parts: usability evidence (per law) + humane-design evidence (per theme).

## State a claim: year, kind of study, sample, finding, caveat

1. Finding: claim in one line.
2. Origin + year: stating law's origin → give year + kind of work; e.g. Hick's Law dates to two origins, 1952 + 1953.
3. Kind of support: name it honestly (list below).
4. Sample + method: only what evidence row gives. Row names no study, sample / number → do not invent one.
5. Number: exactly as reported (rules below).
6. Caveat: contested / weak figure → flag it in row where figure appears.
7. Design consequence: what to do because of it.

Shape: `<finding> — <year>, <kind of study>, <sample> → caveat: <limit> → do: <consequence>`

- Wrong: law's origin cited w/o year → Right: year + kind of work, every time.
- Wrong: figure stated w/o year / kind of support → Right: figure as reported + year + kind of support, so reader can weigh it.

## Kinds of support
Usability (laws): lab reaction-time study · memory experiment · animal study · observational field study · engineering guideline · humorous essay · philosophical principle.
Humane design: research · statistics · historical cases · expert arguments.

|Question|Answer|
|---|---|
|Does law predict?|Only Fitts's Law, Hick's Law + Jakob's Law singled out as predictive models; most other UX laws = descriptive|
|Construct or named effect?|30 UX laws, two families: 7 cognitive constructs + 23 named effects / principles describing / predicting behavior|
|Which are constructs?|Chunking, Cognitive Bias, Cognitive Load, Flow, Mental Model, Selective Attention, Working Memory|
|What does each family do?|Constructs explain behavior; named effects predict / prescribe it|

## Weigh before leaning on it
- UX law = heuristic informing decisions, never mandate.
- User research + project data outrank any UX law.
- High-stakes decision → prefer studies + reports over expert arguments.
- Expert arguments frame decision; they do not prove anything.
- Historical case → premortem prompt (happened to team like ours), not proof same outcome certain.

## Numbers
- Quote exactly as reported.
- Do not round reported number up.
- Do not extrapolate beyond reported number.
- Do not combine figures from different evidence rows.
- Humane-design numbers → as reported, w/ year + kind of support, so reader can weigh them.
- Contested / weak figure → flag it in row where it appears.

## In review feedback
- Pair claim w/ its implication; design-consequence column = instruction to act on.

## Where the evidence lives

Usability evidence = origins, studies + numbers behind each UX law, by theme.
- Use to justify design decisions, cite accurately + check whether evidence supports claim.
- Lives in each law file's Evidence section, reached via [index of the 30 UX laws](../laws/index.md).
- Each row states: claim, specifics (when, method, sample, result), kind of support, design implication.
- Product examples for laws → [applied product cases](product-cases.md).

Humane-design evidence = research, statistics, historical cases + expert arguments backing seven humane-design principles: Respectful, Finite, Empowering, Intentional, Inclusive, Resilient, Transparent.
- Use for review comments, stakeholder arguments + risk sizing.
- One file per theme; table columns: Finding (claim) · Study (year, kind, sample = detail + source) · Strength + caveat · Design consequence (implication).
