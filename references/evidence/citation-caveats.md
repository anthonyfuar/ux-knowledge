# Citation caveats

## Use when
- Check before quoting any number, study, case / origin date.
- Covers known traps: contested numbers, misattributions, misdatings + limits of each body of evidence.
- Two parts: usability evidence by topic (memory numbers, decision laws, motor + time laws, memory of experience, perception + aesthetics, bias + behavior, ethics) · humane-design evidence.

## Part A: usability evidence

## A1. Memory numbers
Applies to: ../laws/millers-law.md, ../laws/working-memory.md, ../laws/chunking.md, ../laws/cognitive-load.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|seven plus or minus two|Miller's Law number = immediate memory span + absolute judgment, not how many items screen may show|never use to cap menu items, tabs / list entries|
|paper behind Miller's Law|its emphasis = chunking|cite for meaningful grouping of information, not magic count|
|date of paper behind Miller's Law|1956; sometimes misdated 1958|cite 1956|
|7±2 vs range of 4–7 chunks vs later estimate of ~four|three different claims|name intended claim; never merge into one rule|
|explainers quote working-memory capacity as 3-4, ~4-6 (1975 estimate), as few as 3 (1999 estimate) or 7|figures differ: come from different tasks + conditions|—|
|3-5 items|estimate of central memory store under tightly controlled conditions|—|
|span in real tasks|varies w/ strategy people use|—|
|fixed capacity|working-memory capacity not fixed: prior knowledge + circumstances shift it|—|
|three types of cognitive load|first treated as additive; now thought to interact + overlap|—|
|popular claims about cognitive load in students + children|several rest on weak citations|—|
|harms of cognitive offloading (e.g. media multitasking, deskilling after AI use)|under active research|present as cautions, not settled effects|

## A2. Decision laws
Applies to: ../laws/hicks-law.md, ../laws/choice-overload.md, ../laws/occams-razor.md, ../laws/pareto-principle.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|Hick's Law origin|rests on 1952 + 1953 work on reaction time to stimuli|—|
|Hick's Law scope|matters most where response speed critical|—|
|fewer options|cutting options too far → abstraction + lost meaning|—|
|recommending one option|remedies choice overload|no license to hide essentials|
|choice overload|harms both satisfaction + speed|where comparison genuinely needed → structure comparison instead of deleting options|
|"overchoice"|term from social commentary, not experiment|never present as experimental proof|
|Occam's Razor|presumes competing options perform equally well|argue for fewest parts still fully working, not cutting for its own sake; never trade clarity for simplicity|
|Pareto split|approximate; holds for many phenomena, not all|use as analytical lens, not measured ratio for your product|

- Wrong: Hick's Law says fewer options always yield better product → Right: it doesn't say so.

## A3. Motor + time laws
Applies to: ../laws/fittss-law.md, ../laws/doherty-threshold.md, ../laws/parkinsons-law.md, ../laws/goal-gradient-effect.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|Fitts's Law formula|several formula variants exist|don't quote one as definitive|
|target size|alone not enough: spacing + reach matter too|—|
|1982 paper behind Doherty Threshold|argues for sub-second system response|—|
|400 ms|popular threshold from later secondary summaries|cite as popular threshold, not as 1982 paper's finding|
|1982 response-time figures: authors|by staff of hardware maker selling faster processors|—|
|1982 response-time figures: design|small samples (5 forecasters, 15 engineers), before/after comparisons, no controls|—|
|fast apps billed as addicting · progress bars helping even when inaccurate · artificial delays raising trust|levers can mislead|weigh against well-being + honesty|
|1968 response-time limits|one scientist's reasoned estimates, explicitly unvalidated|treat as expert guidance, not data|
|often-repeated 34% sales lift from moving add-to-cart control (2010)|single-vendor anecdote from testing-tool company|cite only as weak evidence|
|Parkinson's Law|began as joke line in humorous essay drawn from civil-service experience|observation, not study|
|goal-gradient evidence|began w/ animals: rats running for food|—|
|goal-gradient in humans|2006 field studies support it but involve real rewards; no-reward control group slowed down; customers accelerating more likelier to return|never restate as finding about why people drop out|
|artificial or illusory progress|motivates people but raises honesty concerns|—|

- Wrong: 400 ms target, 2-second "previous standard" + label "addicting" come from 1982 paper → Right: they come from later secondary summaries.
- Wrong: forecasters gained 339% → Right: 3.39× rate (99 → 336 transactions per hour) = ~239% increase.

## A4. Memory of experience
Applies to: ../laws/peak-end-rule.md, ../laws/zeigarnik-effect.md, ../laws/von-restorff-effect.md, ../laws/flow.md, ../laws/selective-attention.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|peak-end evidence|from lab pain (cold water) + medical procedures, not interfaces|transfer to UX = inference|
|wait-screen result at Uber|one company A/B test (2019), 11% drop in post-request cancellations|single in-house experiment, not general effect size|
|cold-water choice study|—|cite: longer trial = one most participants picked; quote no percentage w/o checking 1993 paper|
|Zeigarnik Effect + Von Restorff Effect studies|—|quote no sample size|
|Zeigarnik Effect|replication record mixed; memory benefit for interrupted tasks rests on early work from 1927 + 1935|—|
|flow → performance|link mostly reversed: longitudinal academic study found good performance produced flow more than flow produced performance|—|
|vendor claims about flow (e.g. flow makes people 5x more productive)|lack study details|don't cite|
|tunnel-vision timeline example (2012)|rests on one observed participant|illustration, not effect size|

- Wrong: peak-end rule means duration never matters → Right: retrospective ratings track peak + end more than total.

## A5. Perception + aesthetics
Applies to: ../laws/aesthetic-usability-effect.md, [Law of Prägnanz](../laws/pragnanz.md), ../laws/common-region.md, ../laws/uniform-connectedness.md, ../laws/proximity.md, ../laws/similarity.md, ../laws/von-restorff-effect.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|original aesthetic-usability study|ease of use correlated w/ perceived ease, not actual ease|—|
|usability tests of attractive interfaces|can miss problems: users forgive flaws + rate interface usable|—|
|dates|Prägnanz 1910 · Common Region 1992 · Uniform Connectedness 1994|—|
|Proximity + Similarity|trace to no specific person / study beyond 1923 formalization of perceptual grouping|attribute to Gestalt psychologists generally|
|innate (Gestalt view) vs learned from statistics of real objects (1975 argument)|contested|cite grouping laws as reliable descriptive tendencies, not as processing model (../laws/gestalt-principles-overview.md)|
|evidence for grouping laws|mostly demonstration figures, not controlled UX experiments|say so when citing, e.g. 72-circle example|
|Gestalt therapy|separate field from Gestalt perception research; one scholar argued no substantive relation links them|don't cite as perceptual evidence|
|visual distinctiveness|works only when rare: overuse cancels itself; users may mistake salient blocks for ads; color alone cannot carry contrast; motion contrast can harm motion-sensitive people|—|

- Wrong: attractiveness makes interface usable → Right: it doesn't.

## A6. Bias + behavior
Applies to: ../laws/cognitive-bias.md, ../laws/selective-attention.md, ../laws/paradox-of-the-active-user.md, ../laws/teslers-law.md, ../laws/postels-law.md, ../laws/cognitive-load.md, ../laws/flow.md, ../laws/serial-position-effect.md, ../laws/jakobs-law.md.

|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|knowing about bias|improves how often it gets caught; bias itself stays active|—|
|heuristics|efficient; problem = hidden systematic errors|—|
|where attention filters|models disagree: early + physical, attenuated, or late + semantic|present none as settled|
|memory-selection models|attended + unattended input both pass initial filter, sorted by meaning at second stage → explains why own name breaks through|—|
|resource (capacity) theories|criticized as broad + vague|—|
|ecological view|attention = orienting toward person's goals in environment, not symptom of brain limits|—|
|any one attention model|none explains all of attention|cite each model for part it explains|
|paradox of the active user: origin|1980s studies of how people learned + routinely used word processors + text editors|—|
|paradox of the active user: two groups|novices skip instructions + jump straight in; experienced users plateau: stay w/ methods they know, rarely seek better ones|design for discovery of efficient methods over whole life of use, not only during onboarding|
|manuals|now rare, yet both active-user behaviors (novices skipping instructions, experienced users plateauing) persist|—|
|Tesler's Law|complexity reassigned, never eliminated|pair w/ related observation: simplification may raise users' ambitions instead of lowering their total effort|
|Postel's Law leniency|applies only when input meaning unambiguous|still needs defined limits + clear feedback|
|icon memorability claim|no specific study behind it|treat w/ caution|
|flow idea millennia old, known under different names|some claim it; not established fact|—|
|illustrative company claims: product redesigns; serial-position use credited to Electronic Arts, Nike + Apple|cited w/o data|use as illustrations only|

- Wrong: design for perfectly rational ideal user → Right: rational ideal user = wrong design target.

## A7. Ethics
- Findings making products intuitive can also serve to exploit attention.
- Reported harms of attention-exploiting products: weaker relationships, anxiety, lost sleep.
- Before using UX law to raise engagement → ask: does engagement goal match user's goal?
- Look past usage metrics to qualitative research on product's effect on people's lives.

## Part B: humane-design evidence

## B1. Kind of support
- Several strong-sounding humane-design claims = expert arguments / single cases, not controlled studies → say so when citing.

## B2. Adolescent short-video study + time-spent figures
|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|2021 study of adolescent short-video use (TikTok use disorder)|cross-sectional + non-representative, by its authors' own caution|claim no causation from it|
|what 2021 adolescent short-video study measured|ten-item self-report scale of disordered use, adapted from smartphone-addiction questionnaire; not recorded hours|quote as evidence on compulsive use, not screen time|
|own product metrics|average minutes per person can fall while signs of lost control rise|instrument own product same way as 2021 study; time-spent figure alone neither confirms nor clears any feature|
|Netflix streaming, first half of 2023|over 92 billion hours in one summary, nearly 100 billion in another; offered as sign recommendation-based personalization works|quote range, not single number (decision-fatigue-and-choice.md)|

## B3. Autoplay + endless scrolling
- No humane-design evidence entry measures autoplay / endless scrolling on its own.
- Available research covers overall short-video use, not single mechanics.
- 2023 human-rights-organization report on TikTok's For You feed describes recommender behavior w/o isolating one mechanic.
- Twenty-minute figure in 2024 essay attributed to same 2023 report, but report's own summary carries no time figure → treat as unverified; don't attach it to report.
- How to argue instead:
  1. From mechanism autoplay + endless scrolling share w/ variable reward + removed stopping cue (addiction-and-variable-rewards.md).
  2. From protections already shipped for minors (children-and-adolescents.md): one major video service sets autoplay off + default break + bedtime reminders for under-18s; one short-video app sends no push notifications after 9 pm to users aged 13-15.
  3. Say plainly: no dedicated study cited, instead of stretching one adolescent study across three distinct mechanisms.

## B4. Contested or false stories + interested sources
|Claim or source|Trap|Cite / do|
|---|---|---|
|infrastructure cases (overpasses, film stock)|intent contested|use to argue impact matters regardless of intent, not to assert malice (unintended-consequences.md, safety-and-abuse.md)|
|oak-beams story about New College, Oxford|false|don't use as fact|
|industry personalization statistics|from consulting firm w/ commercial interest in personalization|use to explain personalization's pull, not to justify surveillance (personalization-and-surveillance.md)|
